Makeshift Radio Boards

Both makeshift sets are now hand-wired boards that follow published circuits, wire for wire. On each set the display and its keys sit on a salvaged board bolted on top, joined by a ribbon cable, as you chose.

Both boards are painted. A program checks every painted wire, part leg and solder joint against the circuits below, and both boards pass. Nothing is in the game yet.

Both drawings below are mine, redrawn from the netlists the boards are painted from. Lines that cross without a dot are not joined.

Makeshift two-way · painted

Talking Electronics' four-transistor walkie-talkie (Colin Mitchell, "27MHz Links", Fig. 24): the toy walkie-talkie circuit of the 1980s and 90s. One transistor listens and talks: it is a sensitive receiver while listening and a crystal-locked transmitter while talking. Three transistors amplify. The speaker is the microphone while you talk. One four-pole talk/listen switch changes everything over.

What the plate's controls are

ON THE PLATEIN THE CIRCUIT
PWRThe on/off switch, SW2, between the 9 volt battery and everything else.
VOLThe 10k volume control, VR1. While you talk it also sets how loud your voice goes out.
DIALA knob glued onto the tuning slug of the coil, T1. It tunes what you hear; what you send stays on the crystal.
MICThe talk/listen switch, SW1, a square latching push switch in the key row. It sits latched in while your microphone is on and out while it is off, whether the set is on or not.
CH+ CH- MEM MUTEKeys on the salvaged board, with the display and the red lamp. Its ribbon takes power and ground only.
RANGEStays a strip of masking tape on the board.

Decided

You approved this circuit and MIC as the talk/listen switch.

  • The real circuit works on the 27 megahertz citizens' band. The salvaged display keeps showing the game's channels and frequencies, the same arrangement as the radio's.
  • In the game you still hear the other side while your microphone is on. The real switch would cut the receiver while you talk; the picture can't show that either way.
  • The article's text calls the emitter resistor 330 ohms; its drawing says 390. The board follows the drawing.

The painted board · off, then on

Top: off, microphone off, the talk/listen switch out. Bottom: on, microphone on, the switch latched in. The ribbon leaves the display board at its right edge for the supply and ground runs.

Makeshift radio · painted

The receiver is Philips' own test circuit for the TDA7000 single-chip receiver (data sheet, May 1992, Fig. 4), with an antenna lead where the test generator was, as Philips' own board has it. The amplifier is National's LM386 "minimum parts" circuit. One 220 nanofarad capacitor that is in neither drawing joins them, so the volume control doesn't carry the receiver's output voltage.

What the plate's controls are

ON THE PLATEIN THE CIRCUIT
PWRThe red rocker, SW1, between the 4.5 volt battery and both chips.
VOLThe 10k volume control, VR1, into the LM386.
TUNEA two-gang tuning capacitor salvaged from a pocket radio, CV, in the TDA7000's oscillator.
Display, 1-6, SEEKThe salvaged board, as on the two-way.
  • The data sheet's muting switch is left off. Its "enabled" position is open, so leaving the switch and its 10k off wires the same circuit.
  • The data sheet leaves two oscillator capacitors without values. 33 and 100 picofarads put 88 to 108 megahertz on the dial.
  • The data sheet asks for 22k on the output at 4.5 volts (47k at 9 volts), so the radio runs on three cells.
